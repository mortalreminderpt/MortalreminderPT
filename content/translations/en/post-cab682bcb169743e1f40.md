### Media Access Control (MAC) Layer

The Media Access Control (MAC) layer is responsible for managing access to the shared communication channel in broadcast networks, determining how multiple users can efficiently share the channel, minimizing collisions, and maximizing throughput.

---

### Static Channel Allocation
Static channel allocation assigns channels fixedly to different users, and is suitable for scenarios where the number of users is known and stable.

- **Methods:**
  - **Time Division Multiplexing (TDM):** Divides time into fixed time slots, with each slot assigned to a specific user.
  - **Frequency Division Multiplexing (FDM):** Divides the channel into fixed frequency bands, with each user assigned one band.

**Example:**
FM Radio uses static channel allocation because the number of channels is stable and each frequency band is permanently assigned.

**Advantages:**
- Simple and easy to use
- Fair and highly predictable allocation

**Disadvantages:**
- Inefficient under varying load or bursty traffic
- Lacks the ability to adjust dynamically

---

### ALOHA Protocol
The ALOHA protocol requires no central control and dynamically allocates the channel through retransmission after collisions; its efficiency varies with traffic load.

#### Pure ALOHA
- Users transmit at any time when they have data.
- If a collision occurs, they retransmit after a random delay.
- Simple and effective under low load, but inefficient under high traffic due to frequent collisions.

#### Slotted ALOHA
- Divides time into fixed time slots, and transmission must begin at the start of a slot.
- Improves throughput by reducing the probability of collisions, and is more efficient than pure ALOHA.

**Comparison:**
- Pure ALOHA has lower latency under low load, but a higher collision rate.
- Slotted ALOHA reduces collisions through synchronized transmission, but introduces some latency.

---

### Carrier Sense Multiple Access (CSMA)
CSMA reduces collisions by sensing the channel before transmitting, and includes the following variants:

#### Persistent CSMA
- Continuously monitors the channel.
- Transmits immediately when the channel is idle; if a collision occurs, retries after a random delay.

#### Non-persistent CSMA
- Periodically checks the channel; if the channel is busy, waits a random time and then retries.
- Reduces collisions, but may increase channel idle time.

#### p-Persistent CSMA
- Suitable for slotted channels:
  - When the channel is idle, transmits with probability $ p $;
  - With probability $ 1-p $, defers to the next slot and checks again.
- Seeks a balance between persistence and collision risk.

**Key observations:**
- CSMA outperforms ALOHA under various loads, especially under high traffic.
- The non-persistent and low-probability persistent variants perform better under high-load conditions.

### Collision-Free Protocols
Collision-free protocols completely avoid collisions through orderly channel access.

#### Bitmap Protocol
- Each station declares via one bit whether it has data to send.
- Data is transmitted in order according to the station sequence.
- Efficiency: $ \text{利用率} = \frac{d}{d+N} $, where $ d $ is the frame length and $ N $ is the number of stations.

#### Binary Countdown Protocol
- Stations are ordered by the priority of their binary addresses, and conflicts are resolved bit by bit.
- Efficiency: $ \text{利用率} = \frac{d}{d+\log N} $.

**Vivid analogy:**
Binary countdown is like a "raise-your-hand rule," where participants with higher priority get the chance to speak earlier.

---

### Limited-Contention Protocols
Combines the advantages of contention protocols and collision-free protocols:

- Performs well under low load with short latency (similar to contention protocols).
- Under high load, no collisions need to be handled, improving efficiency (similar to collision-free protocols).

#### Adaptive Tree Walk
- Uses a hierarchical structure to divide colliding stations into smaller groups and resolve them layer by layer.
- Stations move down the "tree" structure until only one station remains and transmits successfully.

**Example flow:**
- Stations initially in collision are grouped, and collisions are reduced layer by layer until all stations transmit successfully.

### MACA Protocol
MACA (Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance) solves the hidden terminal and exposed terminal problems through control messages:

- **Hidden terminal problem:** Collisions occur because nodes cannot see each other.
- **Exposed terminal problem:** Nodes mistakenly believe they cannot transmit simultaneously, reducing efficiency.

**Solution:**
1. The sender sends a "Request to Send (RTS)".
2. The receiver replies with a "Clear to Send (CTS)", notifying nearby nodes.
3. After data transmission is complete, the channel is released via an "Acknowledgement (ACK)".

**Advantages:**
- Avoids collisions from hidden nodes.
- Allows exposed nodes to transmit simultaneously, improving efficiency.

### Ethernet Frame Format and Performance
Ethernet uses the MAC layer for channel access and is widely used due to its simplicity and scalability.

#### Frame format:
- **Preamble:** Used for synchronization.
- **Destination/Source address:** Identifies the sender and receiver.
- **Payload:** Transmits data.
- **CRC checksum:** Detects transmission errors.

#### Performance:
- **CSMA/CD (Collision Detection):** Efficiently utilizes the channel by detecting and resolving collisions.
- Throughput depends on traffic load and performs best under moderate load conditions.

---

### Summary
The MAC layer plays a key role in shared channel access. Choosing the appropriate protocol (static, ALOHA, CSMA, etc.) based on traffic patterns, load, and network requirements seeks a balance among simplicity, efficiency, and fairness.
